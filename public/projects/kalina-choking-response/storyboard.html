Important correction versus older/US-style first aid content (2026-09-06)

Current Australian guidance (ANZCOR, St John Australia) does not recommend abdominal thrusts (the Heimlich manoeuvre) for a conscious adult or child over one year, due to the risk of serious internal injury. The correct technique is up to 5 back blows, then up to 5 chest thrusts (not abdominal), alternating between the two if the obstruction doesn't clear. This is different from some US Red Cross material and from what many support workers may have learned informally, so the course explicitly corrects this misconception rather than assuming it.

Learning Objectives

Objective 1Recognise the difference between mild and severe choking and respond appropriately.
Objective 2Correctly perform back blows and chest thrusts, and understand why abdominal thrusts are not used in current Australian first aid guidance.
Objective 3Identify choking risk factors and follow a person's mealtime management plan to help prevent choking.
Objective 4Know when to escalate to emergency services and how to report a choking incident.

Recognising Choking (hotspot)

Effective coughThe person is coughing forcefully and can still make sound. Their airway is only partially blocked - don't interfere, just stay close and encourage them.
Clutching the throatThe universal sign of choking. Ask "Are you choking?" - if they nod and can't speak, treat it as severe.
Can't speak, cough, or breatheA silent, weak, or absent cough means the airway is completely or severely blocked. This is a medical emergency - act immediately.
Turning blue (cyanosis)A sign of severe oxygen deprivation. Call 000 and continue back blows and chest thrusts without delay.

Mild or Severe? (matching)

MildStill coughing forcefully, can speak or make sound, appears distressed but is moving air.
SevereSilent or very weak cough, cannot speak or breathe, may be clutching the throat, may start to turn blue.
Either can change quicklyA mild obstruction can become severe within moments - keep watching even after encouraging someone to cough.

Responding to Mild Choking (flip cards)

Encourage coughingAn effective cough is the body's best tool for clearing an airway. Encourage the person to keep coughing.
Stay calm and closeStay with the person, speak calmly, and don't leave them alone.
Don't interfereDon't slap their back or attempt back blows while they're coughing effectively - this can push the object further in or worsen things.
Watch for it turning severeIf the cough becomes weak, silent, or stops, or they can no longer breathe, switch immediately to the severe choking response.

Back Blows and Chest Thrusts (tabs)

Back blows

Bend the person well forward, supporting their chest with one hand. Give up to 5 sharp blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand. Check after each blow to see if the blockage has cleared.

Chest thrusts

If back blows don't clear it, give up to 5 chest thrusts: one hand on the person's back for support, the heel of your other hand on the lower half of the sternum. Thrusts should be slower and sharper than CPR compressions. If it still hasn't cleared, keep alternating 5 back blows with 5 chest thrusts until it clears or medical help arrives.

Why NOT Abdominal Thrusts

Current Australian guidance does not use the Heimlich manoeuvre

ANZCOR and St John Australia do not recommend abdominal thrusts for a conscious adult or child over one year - they carry a real risk of broken ribs and internal organ damage. If you've learned the Heimlich manoeuvre elsewhere, Kalina's practice follows the current Australian standard instead: back blows and chest thrusts.

If the Person Becomes Unconscious

Call 000 immediately

If the person stops responding, call an ambulance straight away, or direct someone else to.

Lower them safely and begin CPR

Lower the person carefully to the ground, open their mouth to check for and remove any visible object, and begin CPR following your basic life support training. Do not perform blind finger sweeps - only remove an object you can clearly see.

Choking Risk Factors (flip cards)

DysphagiaSwallowing difficulties significantly increase choking risk. Always follow the person's mealtime management plan exactly.
Cognitive impairmentMay affect a person's ability to chew thoroughly, eat at a safe pace, or recognise when something is unsafe to swallow.
Reduced muscle controlConditions affecting the muscles used in chewing and swallowing increase risk, especially without correct positioning.
Poor positioningSlouching, lying down, or eating in a wheelchair without proper support all increase choking risk.
Eating too quicklyRushed meals, large bites, or not chewing thoroughly are common, preventable risk factors.

Mealtime Management Plans (tabs)

What's in a plan

A mealtime management plan is prepared by a speech pathologist and sets out exactly how a person with swallowing difficulties should be supported: food and fluid texture, positioning, pace, supervision level, and specific precautions.

Food texture modification

Meals may need to be pureed, minced, or softened, and fluids may need to be thickened. Never substitute or guess a texture - follow the plan exactly.

Positioning and supervision

The plan will specify seating position (usually upright, not reclined), and how closely the person needs to be supervised while eating and for a period afterward.

Preventing Choking at Mealtimes (accordion)

Sit uprightAlways support the person to sit upright during meals and for a period afterward, never lying down or slumped.
Small bites and sipsEncourage small amounts at a time, and allow enough time to chew and swallow before the next bite.
Minimise distractionsReduce talking, TV, or rushing during meals so the person can focus on eating safely.
Supervise as requiredFollow the level of supervision set out in the person's plan - some people need to be watched throughout the entire meal.
Follow the modified texture exactlyNever give a person food or fluid in a texture other than what's specified in their plan, even if they ask for it.

Scenario: Supporting a Wheelchair User (scenario-pick)

A participant who uses a wheelchair suddenly can't speak or cough during a meal. What should you do?

Try to lift them out of the wheelchair onto the floor first.
Wait for them to work it out themselves since moving them seems risky.
Lean them forward as far as safely possible in the chair and give back blows, adapting your position and support as needed.
Give abdominal thrusts since back blows are harder in a seated position.

Scenario: Limited Communication (scenario-pick)

A participant with limited verbal communication suddenly looks distressed during a meal, wide-eyed, and stops moving. They can't tell you what's wrong. What should you do?

Assume it's nothing since they haven't said anything is wrong.
Wait a few minutes to see if it passes.
Treat it as possible choking - check their airway and breathing, and respond accordingly.
Only act if they clutch their throat.

Scenario: It Hasn't Cleared (scenario-pick)

You've given 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts, and the obstruction still hasn't cleared. What should you do?

Switch to abdominal thrusts since back blows and chest thrusts haven't worked.
Stop and wait for the ambulance to arrive without doing anything further.
Keep alternating 5 back blows with 5 chest thrusts until it clears or medical help arrives.
Attempt a blind finger sweep of their mouth.

When to Call an Ambulance

Even after the obstruction clears

Call 000 if the person needed back blows or chest thrusts at all, if breathing difficulty continues, if they lose consciousness at any point, or if you have any doubt.

Reporting After an Incident

Always report, even if it resolved

Report every choking incident to your supervisor, regardless of whether an ambulance was called. Document what happened, and flag it for review against the person's mealtime management plan in case anything needs updating.

A participant at the table suddenly stops talking, stands up, and clutches their throat with both hands. What's the right first step?

Ask them to sit back down and relax.
Offer them a glass of water.
Ask "Are you choking?" and check if they can speak or cough.
Immediately begin chest thrusts without checking anything first.
