Choking Response

Recognise choking, respond correctly using current Australian first aid guidance, and prevent choking during mealtimes.

Learning Objectives

By the end of this course, you'll be able to:

  • Recognise the difference between mild and severe choking and respond appropriately
  • Correctly perform back blows and chest thrusts, and understand why abdominal thrusts are not used in current Australian first aid guidance
  • Identify choking risk factors and follow a person's mealtime management plan to help prevent choking
  • Know when to escalate to emergency services and how to report a choking incident

Why It Matters

Choking can become life-threatening within minutes. Many of the people you support face a higher risk of choking than the general population, whether from swallowing difficulties, cognitive impairment, or reduced muscle control. Knowing exactly what to do, and what not to do, genuinely saves lives.

Back Blows and Chest Thrusts

Select each tab to learn the correct technique, step by step.

Bend the person well forward, supporting their chest with one hand. Give up to 5 sharp blows between the shoulder blades with the heel of your other hand. Check after each blow to see if the blockage has cleared.

If back blows don't clear it, give up to 5 chest thrusts: one hand on the person's back for support, the heel of your other hand on the lower half of the sternum. Thrusts should be slower and sharper than CPR compressions. If it still hasn't cleared, keep alternating 5 back blows with 5 chest thrusts until it clears or medical help arrives.

Why NOT Abdominal Thrusts

Current Australian guidance does not use the Heimlich manoeuvre

ANZCOR and St John Australia do not recommend abdominal thrusts for a conscious adult or child over one year - they carry a real risk of broken ribs and internal organ damage. If you've learned the Heimlich manoeuvre elsewhere, Kalina's practice follows the current Australian standard instead: back blows and chest thrusts.

If the Person Becomes Unconscious

Call 000 immediately

If the person stops responding, call an ambulance straight away, or direct someone else to.

Lower them safely and begin CPR

Lower the person carefully to the ground, open their mouth to check for and remove any visible object, and begin CPR following your basic life support training. Do not perform blind finger sweeps - only remove an object you can clearly see.

Mealtime Management Plans

Select each tab to learn more.

A mealtime management plan is prepared by a speech pathologist and sets out exactly how a person with swallowing difficulties should be supported: food and fluid texture, positioning, pace, supervision level, and specific precautions.

Meals may need to be pureed, minced, or softened, and fluids may need to be thickened. Never substitute or guess a texture - follow the plan exactly.

The plan will specify seating position (usually upright, not reclined), and how closely the person needs to be supervised while eating and for a period afterward.

Preventing Choking at Mealtimes

Select each section to expand it.

Always support the person to sit upright during meals and for a period afterward, never lying down or slumped.

Encourage small amounts at a time, and allow enough time to chew and swallow before the next bite.

Reduce talking, TV, or rushing during meals so the person can focus on eating safely.

Follow the level of supervision set out in the person's plan - some people need to be watched throughout the entire meal.

Never give a person food or fluid in a texture other than what's specified in their plan, even if they ask for it.

That's right. Adapt your position and support to the person's seating rather than moving them unnecessarily or switching techniques - back blows and chest thrusts still apply, just adapted for a seated position.

Scenario: Limited Communication

A participant with limited verbal communication suddenly looks distressed during a meal, wide-eyed, and stops moving. They can't tell you what's wrong. What should you do? Select a card.

That's right. Someone with limited verbal communication may not be able to give you the "classic" signs - sudden distress, wide eyes, or stillness during a meal are enough reason to check immediately.

Scenario: It Hasn't Cleared

You've given 5 back blows and 5 chest thrusts, and the obstruction still hasn't cleared. What should you do? Select a card.

That's right. Never switch to abdominal thrusts. Keep alternating 5 back blows with 5 chest thrusts, and call 000 if you haven't already.

When to Call an Ambulance

Even after the obstruction clears

Call 000 if the person needed back blows or chest thrusts at all, if breathing difficulty continues, if they lose consciousness at any point, or if you have any doubt.

Reporting After an Incident

Always report, even if it resolved

Report every choking incident to your supervisor, regardless of whether an ambulance was called. Document what happened, and flag it for review against the person's mealtime management plan in case anything needs updating.

The Best-Practice Standard

ANZCOR Guideline 4 - Airway, and St John Australia's Choking First Aid

The primary Australian first aid protocols for recognising and responding to choking, paired with the NDIS Quality and Safeguards Commission's Practice Alert on dysphagia, safe swallowing, and mealtime management.

anzcor.org - Guideline 4

Always follow the person's own mealtime management plan first. If your own observations ever seem to conflict with what's covered here, check with your supervisor or RN.
